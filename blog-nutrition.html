Eating well on a busy work schedule

Practical meal planning that survives a packed calendar.

FA
Dr. Femi Adeyemi
4 min read Jul 8, 2026
Share

Most nutrition advice assumes a version of life with time to spare — a leisurely grocery trip, an unhurried kitchen, three home-cooked meals a day. For anyone working long or unpredictable hours, that advice quietly gets filed under "someday." The good news is that eating well doesn't actually require that much spare time. It requires a system that survives the days you don't have any.

Here's what tends to hold up when the calendar is packed.

1. Batch-cook the boring, unglamorous base

You don't need to meal-prep every dish for the week — just the slow, unglamorous parts: a pot of grains, roasted vegetables, a tray of baked protein. Do that once, on whichever day you have 45 minutes, and every meal after becomes assembly instead of cooking. The goal isn't a perfect menu; it's removing the decision from a tired weeknight.

2. Anchor every plate to a protein

When a meal is thrown together in five minutes, protein is usually what's missing — and it's what keeps you full enough to skip the 3pm vending machine run. Keep two or three easy sources on hand at all times: eggs, canned beans, rotisserie chicken, Greek yogurt. Build the rest of the plate around whatever's already in the fridge.

"You're not failing at nutrition because you skipped a home-cooked dinner. You're failing at it when there's nothing decent within reach when you're starving and out of time."

3. Stock a shelf, not just a fridge

Keep a small, boring rotation of shelf-stable backups for the days cooking simply isn't happening: canned soup with real vegetables, nut butter, whole-grain crackers, dried fruit. The point isn't that these replace real meals often — it's that they replace the drive-through when nothing else is ready.

4. Don't skip meals to buy back time

Skipping lunch feels like it saves twenty minutes, but it usually costs more later — a bigger, less deliberate dinner, or a string of snacks eaten standing up. A meal you eat in ten rushed minutes still counts. It's rarely worth trading for a meal you don't eat at all.

A note from the clinic

These habits are meant as a baseline, not a prescription. If you're managing a specific condition, allergy, or medication that affects diet, talk to your care team before making major changes.

A busy schedule doesn't have to mean bad nutrition — it just changes what "planning" looks like. Less recipe, more system: a stocked shelf, a batch of something basic in the fridge, and a habit of not letting hunger make the decision for you.
